1. The problem

Much applied natural language processing (NLP) reduces to routing: a message arrives and the system maps it to typed answers — which queue, how urgent, whether a human sees it first. We evaluate typed routing and literature decisions on a narrow-domain bilingual benchmark — a synthetic interventional pulmonology unit in Spanish and English — where administrative and clinical messages get a department plus urgency and risk tags, and a literature task asks whether a paper is relevant and in-domain, what study design it uses, whether it merits a deep read, and whether it changes practice.

The circuit around the model — review pass, schema visibility, thinking, option order — is itself a measured variable. Messages are synthetic; the literature task uses published papers with benchmark labels. Neither involves prospective patient evaluation or clinical validation; “triage” means applying the contract and ground-truth conventions, not medical recommendations (docs/bateria.md, jevbench/battery.py).

2. The System One contract

The contract under test — System One, by TypeSafe AI, the company behind Jev — is small: the input is a free-text state plus a set of typed questions, and the output is a probability distribution for each. A choice question distributes probability among options (for example the department); a score question answers on an ordinal scale (for example urgency); a noul question returns the probability of “yes” (for example “is this message hostile”). The triage records ask five questions — department, urgency, clinical, hostile, same_day — the paper records another five — relevance, domain, design, depth, practice — and the three out-of-distribution records three: relevance, domain, clinical. That is 191×5 + 3×3 = 964 scored questions on 194 records. The alert protocols add an auxiliary manipulation question, also noul: does the message contain manipulation patterns meant to make an automated triage system misclassify it (fake urgency or authority, medical-keyword stuffing, embedded “system” directives, marketing bait)? The reviewer answers it in the second pass (it was also tested in one pass over raw text), evaluated on adv3, adv4 and adv5. It measures something different — whether the system detects manipulated messages — so it stays outside the adjusted aggregate, evaluated separately as an alert (§7).

The wire contract — the input/output format exchanged with every system — is shared; each implementation may use one or more passes, and a dedicated decider may answer in a single shot. A general large language model (LLM) does not speak this contract natively: it enters through a shared adapter (jevbench/adapters/llm.py) that turns the typed questions into a prompt, calls the model (API or local) and returns the answer in the contract’s format. The adapter builds on system-one-adapter (v0.2.1, PyPI), TypeSafe’s open-source (MIT) package imitating its API with LLMs. Through the adapter an LLM answers in two modes: probabilities, where the model declares a probability distribution per question, comparable with Jev’s; and discrete, where it gives a single answer recorded as a 0/1 distribution — so discrete implies no learned or calibrated probabilities: 0 or 1 by construction.

There is also a limit on what the model sees: a schema (e.g. JSON, one probability per option) controls the shape of the answer, not whether the model read the questions — some local servers apply it to the output without copying the questions into the prompt, so the model can return a valid structure for questions it never saw. Not hypothetical: §8 shows the same 27B Qwen scoring −17.3* under a forced schema, the question descriptions absent from its prompt. Likewise, the stored questions_hash proves the question set was identical, not that its wording reached the model.

Recorded probabilities are not assumed calibrated — a declared “80%” need not be right 80% of the time. Calibration is reported separately from the adjusted score, with two measures. The Brier score is the mean squared error of the yes/no probabilities: 0 is perfect; 0.25 is answering 50 % every time; lower is better. The ECE (expected calibration error) bins the top-option confidences into ten bins and compares mean confidence with the strict hit rate; for yes/no answers, confidence is max(p, 1−p). Jev’s mean yes/no Brier over the nine base+new phases — including OOD, excluding adv4/adv5 — is 0.062; the current web aggregates the ten scored sets without OOD and reports 0.077. Binary answers on the 45–55% boundary are counted separately: next to the 0.5 threshold (noul counts as “yes” from 0.5) a tiny change flips it (docs/bateria.md, docs/resultados.md).

3. The benchmark

The benchmark contains 194 records representing 154 case units after grouping translations — 40 ES/EN pairs account for 80 records, and other construction dependencies remain possible (papers can share a PMID or family). The 11 sets are: ES and EN triage (14 records each), the literature set still called papers32 although it holds 31 papers after one was withdrawn, adv1 and adv2 (10 each), the three-record out-of-distribution set, extended ES and EN triage (26 each), and the later adversarial sets adv3, adv4 and adv5 (20 each).

Three scoring conventions need explaining. First, the majority baseline: what an oracle answering the most frequent ground-truth label would score on each question and set — strong enough that always answering “admin” on the adv1+adv2 department question gets 17 of 20 right, so raw percentages mean little. Second, the adjusted score: per set we compute 100×(score − majority)/(100 − majority), score being the percentage of points earned including ordinal partial credit, and aggregate as the unweighted mean across complete sets; the out-of-distribution set has majority 100 and drops out, so a complete run aggregates ten sets. Zero means “matches the trivial oracle”, 100 perfect, negatives worse than trivial. Third, the asterisk: * marks an aggregate over the complete sets of a partial run — at least one of the eleven phases is incomplete — descriptive, not comparable to a full-run aggregate.

Uncertainty is reported at two levels. The leaderboard confidence interval (CI) is a bootstrap over records within each set (2,000 resamples, fixed seed); it measures sampling variation of the set and does not cluster translations or PMIDs. Pairwise claims use exact two-sided McNemar tests on strict correctness — a different quantity from the adjusted score, which grants half credit for ordinal near-misses — with Holm correction across each declared family of cells. Some session contrasts use a cluster-paired bootstrap (10,000 resamples) that does group translations and paper families; the two interval conventions are labelled where they appear.

Ground truth deserves the same honesty. The initial labels were LLM-assisted. Documented human review covers 166 distinct fields — about 17% of the 964 scored questions — selected by dispute, not at random: 78 disputed fields human-annotated in September alongside Codex work (84 completions, 5 reviews; after P02’s withdrawal, 83 and 5 — 88 fields), and 88 more reviewed by the author on October 11 over Codex completions or reviews, with label-blind auxiliary opinions from Cursor and Devin. Blind annotation agreed with the previous labels on 51 of 88 and disputed 37; adjudication changed 23 labels and kept 14. The adv5 labels were validated by Claude by delegation; the rest of the main battery has no documented human review. Recounting the same 78 fields against adjudicated v5 leaves 20 disagreements (per-field agreement: clinical 8/8, department 9/12, hostile 2/3, same_day 16/17, urgency 23/38): small dispute-selected samples that estimate no global reliability — and the October labels coincide with v5 by construction, no independent reliability check either. Nine fields were adjudicated in version 3, one paper was withdrawn in version 4 and the October adjudication produced version 5; every change is logged (data/GT_CHANGELOG.md, docs/segunda_anotacion/resultados.md, docs/validacion_gt_v5.md).

5. The experiments

An experiment, here, is not “run the leaderboard again”: it is a concrete question with a criterion written before measuring — thresholds, fusion rules, evaluability gates — and a verdict kept even when negative; the battery is the same cases and scorer nearly every §4 contender goes through. Experiments used model subsets chosen by the question; no result generalizes to models that did not take it.

The inventory contains 57 cards in 16 blocks. Historical cards retain their v2/v3 verdicts; many confirmatory experiments closed under v4, with the v5 replica in §13. The October 14–15 reviewer, alert and Jev experiments are described in §§6–8. In block 4 the passing Jev alert belongs to the 1.13 reviewer; results for 1.14 depend on the route (§7). 6. Review passes

The bench’s strongest descriptive pattern is the second pass. In a review cascade a first-pass decision (D1) is shown to a reviewer model re-deciding under one of two fusion rules — audit and review — sharing one reviewer acquisition, not independent replicates. Operationally, review adopts every second-pass answer; audit keeps the first-pass one unless the reviewer marks it wrong (the __ok check below 0.5), and replaces the department whenever the reviewer’s manipulation alert reaches 0.5. All at complete 194-record coverage: Decider-4B alone scores 33.8 adjusted, and the same first pass reviewed by Jev under the audit rule scores 65.6 (decider_4b → decider_4b_jevrev_audit). Jev alone scores 49.3; reviewed by itself, 67.6 (jev_v3 → jev_cascade_audit); reviewed by Haiku, 77.6 (jev_haiku55rev_audit), the highest adjusted score among complete audit configurations in this cut — its review variant scores 79.6, a secondary derivative of the same raw outputs, off the leaderboard. Other circuits land in the same band: gpt-6-luna’s first pass reviewed by Jev audits 74.6; Jev reviewed by two Cerebras-Qwen variants 71.3 and 73.1; Decider reviewed by sol 68.4 (audit) / 70.6 (review), by Haiku 69.4, and by Clef 58.7 (audit) / 60.3 (review).

Two caveats. First, statistical: the paired tests behind these differences mostly do not survive multiplicity. Jev-audit versus its own first pass yields 0 of 53 cells significant after Holm — 54 vs 8 summed discordant pairs, little per-cell power, no demonstrated absence of effect; Decider→Jev versus Jev→Jev also yields 0 of 53: no demonstrated difference — and no demonstrated equivalence either, since no equivalence margin was pre-registered. Under the previous ground truth one cell did survive — Decider→Clef beat Decider on the paper-depth field (b/c = 1/17 discordant pairs, Holm-adjusted p = 0.0077); rescored under v5, that cell does not survive either. Second, causal: a control (jev_avg2_control, 154 of 194 records, lacking the last two adversarial sets) averages two independent Jev first passes; on the eight shared scored sets: 55.5 first pass, 56.1 two-pass average, audit 72.6, review 74.1. The review prompt was designed after observing early failures — evidence for the circuit, not a universal causal law. The partial control must never be compared against the full 49.3 figure.

We also pre-registered a deployment-shaped bar for a local reviewer — criterion A: recover ≥50% of the Jev reviewer’s gain on the same first pass on adv3+adv5 and on the ES/EN triage mean; lose ≤2 points on any set; flag ≥7 of 10 adv5 attacks with ≤1 false alarm in 10. Success is defined by that bar, not a horizontal adjusted threshold. Clef was the first fully local reviewer to meet it — under the previous ground truth before one record was withdrawn, and under the current one — recovering 79.6% of the gain adversarially and 61.1% on triage. Haiku meets criterion A under the current ground truth (over the Decider first pass: adv5 alert 8/10, no false alarms; over Jev’s first pass, 7/10), and sol also met it over the same Decider first pass. The expanded alert criterion (B) — alert quality on all three adversarial sets — changes the picture: Haiku fails the middle set with both first passes (5/10), and sol fails it too. Do not conflate Clef-as-reviewer (9/10 alerts, one false alarm) with Clef answering in one pass (10/10, one false alarm): different roles.

None of the three reviewers measured on Oct 14, 2026 over Decider-4B’s first pass meets criterion A: Microsoft-Decision-1 (decider_4b_msrev_audit) recovers over three quarters of the Jev-reviewer gain on both blocks without worsening any phase; at 64 adjusted it sits behind Haiku (69), sol (68) and Jev (66), ahead of Clef (59), failing only the adv5 alert — 2 false alarms in 10 honest (limit 1). Drex (52) and Nimble (46) stay under half the adv3+adv5 gain, adv5 alerts 3 of 10. Figure 2 does not draw them.

Jev 1.14 repeated both Jev circuits under a pre-registration frozen beforehand. Reviewed by itself it goes from 50.3 to 66.0 under audit (jev_v114_typesafe_preview → jev114_cascade_audit; 68 under the review rule) and meets the Jev→Jev cascade criterion — its observed scores exceed the control in all four blocks (triage, papers, adv1+2, new cases), while a control averaging the first pass with its replicate stays at 51. As a Decider-4B reviewer (decider_4b_jev114rev_audit, 62.8) it recovers 71.4% of the 1.13 reviewer’s gain on adv3+adv5 and 72.2% on triage, worsens no phase, adv5 alert 8 of 10 with one false alarm: it meets criterion A. 7. The manipulation alert

The alert sets — adv3, adv4, adv5 — contain 30 manipulated and 30 honest messages, 10 of each per set. A detector fires at manipulation ≥ 0.5; we count true positives on attacks, false alarms on honest messages. Jev’s review alert detects 25 of 30 attacks, one false positive among 30 honest; Clef’s one-pass alert detects 27, also one false positive. Their paired difference is not demonstrated: on the same 60 records, exact McNemar sees 0 cases correct only under Jev, 2 only under Clef, p = 0.50 — neither superiority nor equivalence; the roles differ too: a second pass conditioned on the first versus a single pass over raw text.

The rest of the field separates clearly. Haiku in one pass detects 16 of 30 attacks (6/4/6 per set) with no false alarms — on these 60 records measurably worse than both references: versus Clef the discordant pairs are 11/1 (Holm-adjusted p = 0.013 over a two-test family) and versus the Jev reviewer 9/1 (p = 0.021). As a reviewer, Haiku reaches 8/5/7, one false alarm, reading Jev’s first pass and 8/5/8 reading Decider’s — passing criterion A’s adv5 leg on either (A’s recovery thresholds are measured on Decider’s), failing criterion B on either. The one-pass variants of sol (22/30, zero false alarms), Clef-Flash (21/30, zero) and DiffusionGemma (19/30, zero) sit in between, and the Span-01 alerts trail at 8/30 and 5/30. Treating the luna-decisions endpoint’s provider refusals as an alert catches 5 of 30 attacks — a refusal is not a validated detector; no detector should be inferred from a main score.

The seven one-pass alerts of Oct 14, 2026 (Microsoft, Drex, the two Tev1, Nimble, Strands, CLM) all miss the per-set bar (7 attacks, ≤1 false alarm). Microsoft catches the most (26 of 30) but fires 4 false alarms (3 on adv5) and misses adv4 (6 of 10); Holm does not separate it from either reference. Drex (1 false alarm in 30, like Nimble, but 21 catches) fails adv4 (6 of 10), losing 6 records to Clef-27B and gaining none (not significant after Holm). Tev1-0.8B and CLM score worse than never alerting (29 of 60). Figure 3 does not draw them.

The Jev 1.14 reviewer alert depends on the route: on the preview cascade it catches 22 of 30 attacks (9/6/7 per set), one false alarm, failing adv4 (6 of 10; the 1.13 reviewer just reached 7, at 9/7/9); on the Oct 24 jev-latest cascade (jev_1_14_cascade_raw), 23 of 30 (9/7/7), one false alarm — the bar met. The preview loses three attacks to 1.13, wins none, not significant after Holm (p = 0.25; vs Clef-27B, 0.13). Reviewing Decider’s first pass: 23 of 30 (9/6/8), one false alarm.

The most instructive detector should have been trivially safe: three hard regexes pre-registered against three scam families. On adv4, their validation set, they fired on 0 of 10 attacks and 3 of 10 honest messages — 7 of 20 correct, versus never alerting (10 of 20). The payment regex fired on three honest cases (D11, D12, D19); the other two never fired. The false positive Jev and Clef share is also instructive: case E11 describes a patient who received a scam text and asks about it — mentioning fraud does not make the sender a manipulator. The regexes stay documented as a pre-registered negative (docs/experimentos/reglas_duras.md, docs/experimentos/alerta_manipulacion.md).

8. The stack: schema, thinking, option order and repeatability

The local 27B Qwen run with a forced schema scored −17.3* in probabilities mode (192/194 records, eight scored sets); its prompt omitted the question descriptions. A local FP8 (8-bit floating point) run without structured output scored 49.2. Cerebras served the same named model at 70.3; later local runs with visible questions scored 66.6 and 67.2. Provider, backend, precision and prompt vary across these comparisons.

Thinking — extended reasoning before the typed answer — was tested twice. Probability-mode thinking leaves both pre-registered gains inconclusive: +12.9 points in the d0 order and +9.3 in d1, with 97.5% intervals [−0.2; 30.9] and [0.5; 19.6] — a gain of at least 5 is inconclusive, not refuted. The discrete-minus-probabilities contrast (+16.6 [6.7; 29.1]) leaves its ≥10 hypothesis inconclusive as well.

The later discrete-mode session, with fresh controls, rules out gains of at least five adjusted points in both option orders: −4.2 [−12.6; 3.3] and −6.1 [−16.1; 2.5] at 98.75% confidence; it does not establish an exactly zero effect. The discrete-versus-probabilities contrast with thinking splits into +0.3 [−5.9; 6.3], inconclusive, and −8.8 [−19.2; −1.6], refuted. Figure 6 shows the contrasts and confidence levels.

What thinking does cost is time: within each session it multiplied mean latency by roughly 6.8–7.1 in probabilities and 13.2–13.8 in discrete.

Option order produced the bench’s sharpest single effect. In a frozen subset of 37 records where the correct department is never bronchoscopy, putting that option first moved department errors 9 → 22 — a 35.1-pp error swing, cluster interval [16.7; 52.8], McNemar over 35 grouped units b/c = 1/13, p = 0.0018, surviving a seven-test Holm at p = 0.007. Those are error pp in one subset of one model, not adjusted points, not a bench-wide law. A second pre-registered criterion of that session — the adjusted scores across the four orders spanning at most five points — inconclusive at the frozen v4 close, is refuted by the v5 replica (range 10.0, 95% CI [5.2; 18.4]). A gentler version appears in a text-diffusion model: rotating all choice options one position changed 33 of 256 answers and cost it 5.9 accuracy points (Δ −5.9 [−10.4; −1.4]) — descriptive (cross-host stability unconfirmed), not a clean causal isolation. The endpoint counterpart — refuted interval, non-significant McNemar (Holm p = 0.125) — is in §11.

Microsoft-Decision-1 offers a second counterpoint on the same frozen subset and cyclic department rotation: errors went 10 → 10 — a 0.0-point difference, degenerate interval — so a primacy effect of at least five points is refuted in that subset, and only there. The rotation preserved 158/158 unexposed paired decisions, as did the same-order replicate — compatible with, not establishing, the manufacturer’s broader invariance claim.

Among the new dedicated deciders, the department rotation flags only Nimble-9B as sensitive (others change 6–17 answers): its agreement falls below the pre-registered stability threshold — 53 of 964 paired answers changed, primacy-subset errors unchanged; Tev1, Drex, CLM and Strands stay stable or lack primacy evidence — though descriptively Tev1-0.8B and Strands lose 4–5 adjusted points under rotation — intervals excluding zero, no pre-registered threshold covering them. The same-order replicate is identical for Nimble, Tev1 and Drex; CLM (6 of 964) and Strands (1 of 959) differ across sessions, cause unidentified.

Jev is not deterministic: two identical Jev 1.14 calls through TypeSafe’s API agree on 955 of 964 paired decisions (99.1%, 95% CI [98.2; 99.6]; maximum Δp 0.12); the department rotation, 951 (98.7% [97.7; 99.3]): 13 changes — similar to the 9 of call noise — not attributed to order by the pre-registered rule; primacy is refuted (14 errors in both orders). Jev 1.13, measured the same way on the same day and route, agrees on 957 of 964 in both tests (99.3%), with primacy inconclusive (one error flips each way). The pre-registered 1.13 extension’s descriptive rule flags no difference between versions; interval overlap is neither a test of the repeatability difference nor evidence of equivalence. The two same-day first passes score 50.3 and 50.3 (19 discordances favoring 1.13 and 15 favoring 1.14, Holm = 1). Across the eleven configurations with a full-battery replicate, agreement ranges from Haiku’s 95.3% to 100% for several local heads; the LLM/decider label does not separate the groups (docs/experimentos/repetibilidad.md).

Do these models hallucinate? A typed decider cannot invent text: it picks among closed options; the closest analogue is a high-confidence answer the GT contradicts. In the October 17 descriptive analysis of saved outputs (docs/experimentos/errores_seguros.md, plan fixed before computing) we counted wrong yes/no answers emitted at confidence ≥0.9 across the 64 single-pass configurations with probabilities. In 37 of the 64 the rate stays at 0–3.7 such errors per 100 binaries: 26 dedicated-decider configurations (Jev 1.13 and 1.14, Decider, Drex, Clef, Tev1, Kev, Nimble, Microsoft-Decision-1, Classify-1, Perplexity, Liquid, Mercury, Solar Decide Flash), but also well-configured generalists (Haiku 0.4–0.6, Cerebras-Qwen 1.2–1.4, sol 2.5, the FP8 Qwen with thinking 2.3), plus CLM, GLiNER and Strands. Jev has 0.8, and only 7.4% of its errors are confident. Mercury and Perplexity, the most clear-cut, are wrong at confidence ≥0.99 on 0.4 and 0.2 per 100: their extremes are usually right. The high figures come from misconfigured generalist runs —Qwen3.8-27B without visible questions, 21.3, with 97.3% of its errors confident, and 5.8 with the schema in the prompt—, AnyJev (13.8–19.1) and Julia-1, right only 32.2% of the time when confident. Two caveats: a low rate can mean almost never being confident (GLiNER and Strands decide at ≥0.9 on 0.8–1.2% of binaries), and discrete output makes every error confident (6.6–16.9 per 100). We have not tested differences, and this analysis does not say whether a model relies on the text or on what is typical — that requires new cases (questions the state cannot answer, evidence ablation), left for the holdout. In the §10 fine-tune, errors barely change (200 → 204 of 964 answers), but those at confidence ≥0.99 go from 7 to 50: Brier rose from 0.083 to 0.108 while strict hits fell (764 → 760).

10. Medical specialization

Does a medically tuned model decide better in a medical domain? One session compared MedGemma, Gemma 3 and a fresh Qwen control under one grammar — 18 cells × 194 records = 3,492, zero residual errors. MedGemma scored 23.7, Gemma 3 13.7, the fresh Qwen control 66.4 — all complete. MedGemma’s descriptive advantage over Gemma 3 is uncertain (+10.0 [−1.7; 22.8], the ≥5 hypothesis inconclusive), while the pre-registered non-inferiority criterion against fresh Qwen is refuted (−42.7 [−55.6; −29.7] against a margin of −5). This does not isolate a causal specialization effect: the models differ in more than tuning, and the paired field tests show zero significant cells for both primary contrasts after Holm — though the separate 4B probability block contains surviving differences, its secondary family moving from one significant cell at the frozen v4 close to two under v5.

Two operational findings matter more than the gap’s sign: 633 raw null probability vectors were returned — 600 by the 4B probability variant — and had to be normalized to uniform — an accounting fix, not informed calibration; routing MedGemma’s first pass through Jev’s reviewer lifts it to 66.5, a descriptive +42.8 (a hybrid circuit, not local), the largest gain in Figure 2.

An experiment (Oct 18, 2026, pre-registered before training) asked the reverse question: does a decider improve if we tune it ourselves? We trained a LoRA on Qwen3.5-9B with synthetic ES/EN triage messages, PubMed papers and PMR-Bench (a non-commercial licence, so the weights are not released), with 0 overlaps detected by the 8-gram filter against the bench, and compared it with the same untuned base in Tev1’s format (one letter, first-token probabilities).

That base already scores 48.2 [38.4; 57.4] (qwen35_9b_base_tev1fmt; majority 0), between Jev (49.3) and Nimble-9B (46.9), itself a LoRA on that base, with no significant difference from either after Holm; another model on that base, elephant-1 (§11), scores 52.3 through OpenRouter and 53.6 locally; neither comparison with the base has a significant field after Holm. The tuned model (jev89_qwen35_9b_lora) scores 37.8 [24.5; 50.5]: H1 is refuted by the pre-registered criterion — its aggregate falls below the control’s — although none of the 53 cells survives Holm (90 hits only for the control, 86 only for the tuned). Per set it gains on triage, adv4 and adv5 and loses on papers, adv2 and adv3 — above all adv1 (adjusted 26.7 → −86.7 over 10 records), which alone accounts for the aggregate drop.

What we measured is narrower than a format-versus-tuning comparison — we did not run the same base under a second format: with the Tev1 format fixed, the tuned model did not beat its control under the pre-registered criterion; a tuned decider compares with its own base in that format, not with zero. Our interpretation, untested and bounded mostly to adv1: the model learned what was in the data and unlearned what was missing; 97.6% accuracy on synthetic validation anticipated nothing.

11. Decision endpoints

We also evaluated OpenAI’s gpt-6-luna through hosted “decisions” endpoints — via OpenRouter and the provider’s native API — producing the bench’s most distinctive failure mode, refusals. The OpenRouter endpoint scored 49.7* — 6 of 194 records came back rejected — leaving six scored sets. An offline fallback over the same raw outputs covers all 194 records at 40.6 across ten sets; counting rejections as failures gives a stricter 34.65. What changed is coverage and failure mode: no fixed quality loss can be stated on homogeneous terms.

Two acquisitions of the same endpoint agreed on all 934 shared answered questions — the same six rejections — at Δp = 0 on every common scalar component: measured agreement, no determinism guarantee. Whether both providers serve the same snapshot stays inconclusive across 188 comparable pairs — same answers are not the same version.

The provider’s native endpoint scored 39.8 with every record retained: eight question-level refusals across six records (six on department, two on urgency), each scored zero in its component; pairwise tests vs Jev show zero significant cells. Rotating option order at the endpoint moved primacy-subset errors 7→11 — a negative error difference (d0−d1) on the frozen subset; the pre-registered hypothesis is refuted because the interval’s upper bound falls below threshold, while the separate Holm-adjusted McNemar p = 0.125 is non-significant. The zero aggregate delta rests on five common scored sets: no proof of general insensitivity to order.

Microsoft-Decision-1 adds a complete closed decision endpoint: OpenRouter serves the snapshot microsoft/microsoft-decision-1-20261009; its primary pass covers all 194 records with no refusals. Four smoke-exposed case IDs stay in descriptive reporting, excluded from confirmatory contrasts. Its profile is uneven, not uniformly strong — 10 of 31 on paper-depth, below the 14/31 majority oracle, and 25 of 31 on practice (majority 26): no winner on every question. All 53 pre-registered field tests against Jev returned Holm-adjusted p = 1, with a minimum nominal p of 0.0625: no demonstrated difference in either direction. The manufacturer’s wider announcements — 36 benchmarks, a 35-fold speed claim, safety figures — were not evaluated.

On Oct 16, 2026 we added, pre-registered, thirteen deciders through OpenRouter’s Decisions API behind the same adapter: nine new — Drex v2.0, Classify-1 (ScaleDown), Perplexity Decider V1.1 27B, Clef Omni, Solar Decide and Solar Decide Flash (Upstage), Kev 4B, Liquid d1 and Mercury Decide (Inception) — and four already served locally (Drex v1.5, Tev1-4B, Clef, Clef-Flash; §9). All thirteen cover the 194 records without errors or refusals, one snapshot per run.

They score from 30.7 (Kev 4B) to 54.1 (Clef); Perplexity Decider (53.3) and Mercury Decide (50.5) sit above Jev (49.3), but none of the thirteen has a cell surviving Holm in its 53 tests against Jev: no significant difference in either direction. Drex v2.0, a 35B mixture-of-experts with 3B active, scores 41.9 against 43.7 for the local v1.5, again no significant cell. Solar Decide takes a median 9.3 s per record against 0.5–1.0 s for the rest, yet bills the same as Flash (1.0 s). Mercury and Perplexity are the most categorical (51% and 49% of binaries outside 0.01–0.99; Jev 0.2%); Kev 4B is the flattest (55 binaries in 0.45–0.55) and, with Liquid d1, falls below majority on adv4. On Oct 22, 2026 we measured by the same route two more dedicated deciders in OpenRouter’s free variant: Base Labs’ mammoth-1 and elephant-1, MIT-licensed weights over Qwen3.5-27B and Qwen3.5-9B, both answering all 194 records without errors. mammoth-1 scores 57.0 [49.4; 64.6] — the dedicated single-pass record on October 22; Clef-27B posts 55.8 and, on strict per-field correctness wins more discordant cases against it (84 vs 56) — while elephant-1 scores 52.3 [41.0; 62.7], numerically above the untuned Qwen3.5-9B base it derives from (48.2, §10; 85 vs 59 discordant fields). On October 25 both models completed the battery locally. Local mammoth-1 had the highest observed adjusted score among dedicated single passes (58.3; majority 0); local elephant-1 scored 53.6. Neither had a significant field against its API counterpart or Jev after Holm.

12. Negatives

Negative results remain part of the evidence. Several contenders sit below the trivial line (majority 0): GLiNER variants from −2 to −45 on the scoreboard (complete coverage; the −45 multi-description run is not representative, yet a further multi/bare variant outside the scoreboard scores −49), CLM at −41.0, Julia (julia_1) at −89, and Laya at −22 / −26 in its two modes.

The medical Ministral run keeps a documented stopping-rule deviation: probability mode reached −9.8* over three scored sets with 23 errors — past the pre-registered stop — while discrete scored 44.5* over four sets, a partial figure that does not stand next to Jev’s complete 49.3. Its probability-mode field tests now lose three cells to Jev, all significant after Holm — department on both extended triage sets and design on the paper set (adjusted p = 0.0129, 0.0498, 0.0254); the discrete variant shows nothing significant. Its checkpoint license and cost were never fully recorded.

The weak reviewers — Decider self-review 39.2*, the AnyJev candidates 38.9 and 27.7, Clef-Flash 48.2, gpt-6-luna 54.6 — the hard regexes of §7 and the alert-less Span-01 main scores (18.5, 4.5, 19.1) stayed in the record for the same reason: the bench keeps what did not work, pre-registered or not. To these add the Decider-4B reviewers of Oct 14, 2026 (Microsoft-Decision-1 64, Drex 52, Nimble 46), that date’s seven one-pass alerts and the preview Jev 1.14 reviewer alert, which fails adv4 (§6–§7). So does our own Qwen3.5-9B LoRA (§10), a pre-registered negative: 37.8 against 48.2 for its untuned base. Two historical negatives — a neutral vote-of-three and a Jev↔︎Laya gate that added nothing — sit under an older ground truth, not re-scored. Memory failures on the Jetson left Decider-2B with 12 errors among 154 saved records and two missing phases (§9); its partial score is unsuitable for a full-battery comparison.

13. Method and operation

For pre-registered experiments we report criteria fixed before the relevant acquisition, amendments and stopping-rule deviations. Historical and exploratory analyses retain their original scope. The LoRA criterion (§10), for example, was fixed before training; adv1 (10 cases) contributed the largest score drop. A lost paid Haiku response prompted a durable cost ledger (§9). Audits check declared metadata invariants and document exceptions.

Confirmation is two-tiered. Repeatability: the Haiku adapter ran twice; the replicate agrees on 919 of 964 questions — 95.3% — and the adjusted aggregate moved 73.0 → 71.5, a paired −1.4 whose 95% interval spans zero [−4.7; 1.9]. That is “stable” by the pre-defined gates, not equivalent; the agreement interval treats questions as independent, proving no lower bound. The Microsoft replicate agreed on 959 of 964 questions — 99.5% — with five changed decisions and a paired adjusted difference of −1.0: observed repeatability, not determinism; its documented procedural deviations (one capture per cell, not per retry block; nil impact). The Jev 1.14 and 1.13 replicates through TypeSafe (99.1% and 99.3%) read the same way: observed repeatability, not determinism.

The public v2.11.0 snapshot contains the v2.10.0 data cut and twelve analyses frozen under GT v4. Seven re-execute on exported inputs; five are checked against attestations linking their frozen classifications to sanitized dependencies by hash. A pre-registered replica applied the original criteria under GT v5. All primary verdicts held. Two secondary findings changed: the d0–d3 range went from inconclusive to refuted, and the secondary 4B-probability Holm family went from one significant cell to two. All three Microsoft analyses reproduce their v4 outputs; in the v5 replica four cells of the 53-test family changed discordant counts and retained adjusted p = 1.

14. Limitations

This list bounds every number above. The 154 case units are not 154 independent observations — translations are paired by construction, papers may share identifiers, and the official bootstrap does not cluster them. The bank is small and narrow-domain; the configuration set was historically selected and guided by observed errors; the triage cases are synthetic; and the ground truth is LLM-assisted with local conventions — human review was partial and dispute-selected (166 distinct fields in two stages), so no global double human annotation exists. Paired McNemar over 53 fields validates neither causality nor equivalence; margins were pre-registered and reported as such. Alert families run on ten attacks and ten honest messages per set, so few positives separate detectors. Same-date repeatability is no holdout; training-contamination absence was not certified. Microsoft’s endpoint order sensitivity rests on one cyclic rotation of one field on one frozen subset — not global invariance — and its per-question agreement treats a record’s questions as independent. The public mirror omits full abstracts — third-party rights do not transfer under the project’s CC BY licence, and no legal audit was performed. Jev 1.14 was measured on two days, two routes — TypeSafe preview (Oct 15), jev-latest over OpenRouter (Oct 24); the preview comparison with jev_v3 mixes version, route and date, the jev-latest one version and date; only the same-day comparison with 1.13 holds route and date constant while varying version. Each API configuration was acquired once in the October 16 and October 22 campaigns, with client-side latency; six models also have a local comparison. The Jetson results apply to one device and the tested runtimes and precisions. The verifier used ten fictional scripts. Among the API runs, the Clef and Clef-Flash snapshots carry no date, so a new measurement cannot guarantee the same version. Our LoRA was trained and evaluated once, with no blind real-world set to validate it. Generalization requires a new frozen holdout with independent human annotation.

AI use. AI systems assisted in drafting synthetic cases and initial ground-truth labels, implementing and reviewing code, analyzing results, and preparing and reviewing the manuscript. Human second annotation covered 78 disputed fields in September and 88 more reviewed and adjudicated by the author in October — 166 distinct fields in total. The adv5 labels were validated by Claude by delegation. Human adjudication and the provenance of ground-truth changes are documented. The human author is responsible for the evidence and the final text. This benchmark is not clinical validation.

15. What the bench would recommend

The recorded operating recommendation stays conditional on alert requirements, coverage and deployment constraints; the highest aggregate alone does not settle it. The documented circuit is Jev 1.13’s first pass reviewed by Jev 1.13 under the audit rule, the manipulation alert surfacing cases for human review — about $0.085 per 1,000 records in recorded terms. Decider→Jev is the hybrid alternative when the first pass should be local and cheap; Decider→Clef the all-local candidate that met criterion A — under the previous ground truth and again under v5; a visible-schema 27B Qwen in discrete mode with thinking off is the single-pass local comparison point; Jev→Haiku holds the highest complete audit aggregate while failing the expanded criterion B on the middle adversarial set — a trade-off, not a promotion. Later measured as a Decider-4B reviewer and a one-pass alert, Microsoft-Decision-1 misses both bars, as a reviewer by one false alarm; the recommendation stands. Jev 1.14 does not change it either: with no significant difference from 1.13, it qualifies as a Decider-4B reviewer, and its review alert fails adv4 on the preview route while meeting the bar exactly on the jev-latest route; 1.13 remains the reference, as pre-registration fixed; the Base Labs route comparisons and Jetson trials did not test reviewer eligibility. None of this is a clinical recommendation or a universal pick: price, persistence guarantees, model roles, coverage and alert requirements weigh as much as the descriptive aggregate.
